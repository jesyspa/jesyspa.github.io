Lately I've been watching some of [Kane Baker][0]'s videos, for
instance on [The Problem of the Many][1] and on the matter
of [Ordinary Objects][2].
I've found that often the questions being asked just don't
make sense in my mental framework, or have obvious answers
that the videos don't address.
In this post, I'll outline my philosophical position and
show how these questions fit or don't fit into it.
Any feedback is very welcome; I am not a professional
philosopher by any stretch, and this is all just my idle
musings.

I'd like to thank Rafer45 from Dr. Baker's Discord server
for his questions and comments on the matter, which have
helped me nail my position down.

(Suggestions for a better name than "best-effort realism" are
also much appreciated.)

# Ordinary objects

Do ordinary objects, such as tables, trees, cups, exist?
A realist would say that yes, there are things in reality
that correspond to these things.
An antirealist would say that no, they do not: that only the
underlying particles exist but the objects themselves do
not.

My position is that the realist is essentially correct, but
that the interpretation of the question requires some care
to ensure the pitfalls presented by the anti-realist are
avoided.
Once done, the tools given by this interpretation are also
useful for resolving other philosophical questions.

# Key idea

We understand the world in terms of a model of it that we
have in our minds.
All statements we make about the world, even to ourselves,
are made in terms of this model.
Any observations we have are in terms of the model as well.
However, just because they are in terms of the model does
not mean that they agree with it.
Our model is hence at once both a language for making
statements and an idea of which statements in this language
are true.
The two cannot be distinguished, because the latter shapes
how our observations are phrased in the former.

(Example: when we look at a text, we perceive it as being
composed of letters, words, sentences, etc.  These are the
concepts in which we operate, but these concepts only exist
in as far as we have an understanding of how they relate to
each other and to reality.)

Nevertheless, our statements are about the world: they carry
meanings that can turn out to be true (when the world
behaves according to our model) or false (when the world
turns out to be different).

We do not need direct experience to discover whether our
statements are true or false, as we can perform inference.
This inference is dependent on the accuracy of our model,
but so is everything: everything we learn, we learn in terms
of our model, after all.
